Research Quality & Evidence Standards

1. Purpose

This document defines the standards for conducting reliable, evidence-based research.

The goal is not simply to collect information, but to determine:

- What is actually known.
- How it is known.
- Which sources support it.
- How reliable the evidence is.
- What remains uncertain or disputed.
- Where the available evidence is insufficient.

Research must prioritize accuracy, transparency, intellectual honesty, and traceability over appearing comprehensive or confident.

---

2. Research Integrity

Follow these principles throughout every investigation:

1. Never invent facts, statistics, studies, quotations, authors, dates, or references.
2. Never fabricate URLs, citations, research papers, or source titles.
3. Never present assumptions as established facts.
4. Never hide evidence that contradicts the main conclusion.
5. Never exaggerate the significance of a discovery.
6. Never claim that something is rare, unknown, or overlooked without reasonable evidence.
7. Never imply that research is exhaustive when the search coverage was limited.
8. Never confuse an explanation that sounds convincing with one that is supported by evidence.

If information cannot be verified, explicitly say so.

A transparent knowledge gap is better than a fabricated answer.

3. Source Hierarchy

Evaluate sources according to their relevance, credibility, originality, and methodological quality.

Use the following hierarchy as a starting point, not an absolute ranking.

Level 1: Primary Sources

Examples:

- Original research papers.
- Official technical specifications.
- Programming language specifications.
- Original documentation.
- Government datasets and reports.
- Patents.
- Historical documents.
- Original statements from creators.
- Official standards and RFCs.
- Direct experimental results.

These sources provide direct evidence or original information.

However, primary sources are not automatically correct or unbiased. Examine their methodology, limitations, context, and potential conflicts of interest.

Level 2: Authoritative Secondary Sources

Examples:

- Systematic reviews.
- Meta-analyses.
- Academic textbooks.
- University publications.
- Reputable scientific institutions.
- Established technical publications.
- Expert-written research reports.

Use these sources to understand broader findings, context, and relationships between original studies.

Level 3: Expert Commentary

Examples:

- Technical talks.
- Interviews with original creators.
- Expert lectures.
- Engineering discussions.
- Professional conference presentations.
- Detailed expert analyses.

These sources can provide valuable explanations and practical insights.

Distinguish expert interpretation from independently verified facts.

Level 4: Community Knowledge

Examples:

- Reddit discussions.
- Stack Overflow.
- GitHub issues and discussions.
- Developer forums.
- Specialized communities.
- Practitioner experiences.

Use these sources to discover:

- Real-world problems.
- Unusual behavior.
- Common mistakes.
- Workarounds.
- Practical limitations.
- Experiences that formal documentation may not discuss.

Community experience is useful evidence of reported experience, not automatic proof of a general technical claim.

Level 5: General Internet Content

Examples:

- Personal blogs.
- SEO articles.
- Social media posts.
- General educational websites.
- AI-generated articles.

Use these sources cautiously.

They can help discover terminology and potential research directions, but important claims should be verified through stronger independent evidence.

4. Source Evaluation: CRAAP Test

Evaluate important sources using five dimensions.

4.1 Currency

Determine:

- When was the source published?
- When was it last updated?
- Is the information still relevant?
- Has the technology, research, or understanding changed?
- Does the claim depend on a specific version or historical period?

Do not automatically reject old sources.

An original historical document may be more valuable than a recent article discussing the same event.

4.2 Relevance

Ask:

- Does the source directly address the research question?
- Does it provide evidence or merely repeat an explanation?
- Is its depth appropriate?
- Does it discuss the exact version, environment, or context being investigated?

4.3 Authority

Investigate:

- Who created the source?
- What qualifications or experience do they have?
- Are they connected to the subject?
- Does the organization have a credible reputation?
- Are authorship and institutional responsibility identifiable?

4.4 Accuracy

Check:

- Is the claim supported by evidence?
- Can its information be independently verified?
- Are references provided?
- Is the methodology explained?
- Are important limitations acknowledged?
- Does the source distinguish facts from opinions?

4.5 Purpose

Determine why the source exists.

Possible purposes include:

- Education.
- Scientific research.
- Marketing.
- Product promotion.
- Entertainment.
- Political persuasion.
- Financial gain.
- Personal opinion.

Identify possible conflicts of interest without automatically assuming that a source is false because it has an incentive.

5. Source Discovery and Diversity

Do not depend on one search engine, one publication, or one type of source.

Use different research paths depending on the subject.

Academic Research

Potential sources:

- Google Scholar.
- Semantic Scholar.
- PubMed.
- JSTOR.
- ScienceDirect.
- ResearchGate.
- Consensus.

Technical Research

Potential sources:

- Official documentation.
- Language specifications.
- Standards organizations.
- RFC documents.
- GitHub repositories.
- Original engineering blogs.
- Issue trackers.
- Conference presentations.

Historical Research

Potential sources:

- Original documents.
- Archives.
- Historical books.
- Academic historians.
- University collections.
- Interviews and recorded statements.

General Research

Potential sources:

- Government publications.
- Universities.
- Reputable institutions.
- Independent research organizations.
- Subject-matter experts.

These are discovery resources, not automatic endorsements of everything they contain.

6. Cross-Verification

Important claims should be checked against multiple independent sources whenever possible.

For every significant claim:

1. Find the original source when available.
2. Locate independent supporting evidence.
3. Check whether other credible sources report the same information.
4. Search for contradictory evidence.
5. Examine whether the sources depend on the same original reference.
6. Determine whether the evidence supports the exact wording of the claim.

Independence Matters

Five articles repeating the same unsupported statement do not constitute five independent confirmations.

Trace claims back to their original evidence.

Avoid circular citations, where multiple sources cite one another without providing independent support.

When Sources Disagree

Do not arbitrarily choose the source that supports the preferred conclusion.

Instead:

- Identify the disagreement.
- Explain what each source claims.
- Compare their methodologies and evidence.
- Examine publication dates and relevant versions.
- Identify differences in definitions or contexts.
- State what can and cannot be concluded.

Sometimes the disagreement itself is an important research finding.

---

7. Evidence Classification

Classify significant findings using the following categories.

Verified Fact

A claim directly supported by reliable, relevant evidence.

Supported Interpretation

A reasonable interpretation based on available evidence, but not directly established as an indisputable fact.

Hypothesis

A possible explanation or idea that requires further investigation or testing.

Disputed Claim

A claim for which credible sources provide conflicting evidence or interpretations.

Unknown

Information that cannot currently be established with sufficient evidence.

Do not silently promote a hypothesis into a verified fact.

---

8. Confidence Calibration

Match the confidence of every conclusion to the strength of its evidence.

Use language carefully:

Evidence status| Appropriate language
Strong evidence| The evidence demonstrates...
Multiple supporting sources| Multiple independent sources indicate...
Reasonable interpretation| This suggests...
Limited evidence| Available evidence indicates...
Unverified possibility| One possible explanation is...
Conflicting evidence| Sources disagree about...
Insufficient information| This remains uncertain...

Avoid expressions such as:

- Everyone knows.
- It is universally accepted.
- Scientists have proven.
- Experts agree.
- This is definitely the reason.

Unless the evidence genuinely justifies them.

---

9. Avoiding Research Bias

Actively search for information that could challenge the current understanding.

Confirmation Bias

Do not search exclusively for evidence supporting the initial assumption.

Selection Bias

Do not select only the sources that produce a convenient conclusion.

Survivorship Bias

Do not study only successful technologies, companies, experiments, or solutions.

Investigate failures and abandoned alternatives.

Authority Bias

Do not accept a claim merely because a famous person or institution made it.

Recency Bias

Do not assume newer information is always better than older information.

Popularity Bias

Do not confuse popularity with technical correctness or scientific validity.

Availability Bias

Do not assume that easily accessible information represents the full state of knowledge.

Commercial Bias

Consider whether financial incentives influence the source's conclusions.

---

10. Detecting Weak and Misleading Information

Be especially cautious of:

- Articles without identifiable authors.
- Unsupported statistics.
- Fake academic references.
- Studies with unclear methodologies.
- Outdated technical tutorials.
- Content that repeatedly paraphrases other articles.
- Marketing claims presented as scientific conclusions.
- Screenshots without original sources.
- Anonymous claims presented as confirmed events.
- AI-generated content containing fabricated references.
- Articles using impressive terminology without explaining the underlying mechanism.

Do not reject a source solely because it is unfamiliar.

Evaluate its actual evidence.

11. Technical Research Verification

When investigating programming languages, software, frameworks, databases, protocols, or hardware, apply additional verification standards.

11.1 Separate Standards from Implementations

Distinguish between:

- What the official specification defines.
- What a particular implementation does.
- What a specific runtime or browser supports.
- What developers commonly observe.
- What is considered best practice.

These are not necessarily identical.

11.2 Version Awareness

Always identify relevant versions when behavior may change.

For example:

- JavaScript language features.
- ECMAScript editions.
- Node.js versions.
- Browser compatibility.
- Framework versions.
- Database releases.
- API versions.

Do not generalize behavior from one version to all versions.

11.3 Under-the-Hood Accuracy

When explaining internal mechanisms:

- Prefer official specifications and implementation documentation.
- Identify the exact environment being discussed.
- Distinguish conceptual models from actual implementation details.
- Avoid inventing internal operations.
- Explain when implementation details differ between engines or platforms.

11.4 Practical Verification

When possible, verify technical claims through:

- Small reproducible experiments.
- Official test suites.
- Source code inspection.
- Benchmark methodology.
- Documentation examples.
- Real execution in the relevant environment.

If testing is unavailable, clearly label the explanation as theoretical or untested.

Never claim to have executed code or performed an experiment that did not actually happen.

11.5 Code Accuracy

If providing code:

- Check syntax and logical consistency.
- Identify required dependencies.
- Explain environment requirements.
- Distinguish pseudocode from executable code.
- Mention important edge cases.
- Avoid presenting an illustrative example as production-ready software.

---

12. Scientific and Quantitative Claims

For scientific, medical, economic, statistical, or other quantitative topics:

- Identify the study population and sample size when relevant.
- Examine the research design.
- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Identify confounding variables.
- Examine uncertainty and confidence intervals when available.
- Consider replication and independent confirmation.
- Check whether the conclusion extends beyond the studied population.
- Distinguish preliminary findings from established consensus.

Do not turn a single study into a universal rule.

Do not treat statistical significance as proof of practical importance.

When evidence is limited, describe the limitations explicitly.

---

13. Historical Accuracy

When investigating origins and historical development:

- Prefer original records when available.
- Verify dates and names through reliable historical sources.
- Distinguish invention from earlier related ideas.
- Distinguish the first prototype from public release.
- Distinguish original creators from later contributors.
- Avoid assigning sole credit when development involved multiple people.
- Preserve historical context.
- Identify uncertainty in disputed historical accounts.

Do not invent a precise origin date when the development was gradual.

---

14. Citation and Reference Standards

Every important external factual claim should have an identifiable supporting source.

Citation Rules

1. Cite sources close to the claims they support.
2. Use sources actually accessed during research.
3. Never fabricate a citation.
4. Never cite a source that does not support the associated claim.
5. Prefer original sources for technical and historical claims.
6. Use independent sources for cross-verification.
7. Include publication dates when relevant.
8. Include authors and organizations when available.
9. Link directly to the most relevant source page.
10. Preserve enough information for another researcher to locate the evidence.

Citation Quality

A citation is not decoration.

It must provide a genuine path to verification.

Avoid vague references such as:

- According to research.
- Experts say.
- Studies show.
- Many websites report.

Identify the actual evidence.

Reference Information

Where available, record:

- Title.
- Author or organization.
- Publication date.
- URL or DOI.
- Source type.
- Relevant version.
- Main contribution.
- Important limitations.

Never invent missing bibliographic details.

---

15. Research Log and Reference Management

Maintain a structured record of the investigation.

For each useful source, record:

Field| Description
Source| Title and URL
Author| Person or organization
Date| Publication or update date
Type| Primary, secondary, expert, community
Main contribution| What it establishes or explains
Evidence| Important findings or claims
Limitations| Weaknesses or missing information
Reliability| Assessment based on evidence
Research status| Inspected, useful, verified, rejected

A research log should make it possible to understand how conclusions were reached.

Do not collect sources merely to increase the reference count.

Each source should contribute meaningful evidence, context, or a different perspective.

---

16. Evidence Strength

Evaluate evidence according to the nature of the research question.

Consider:

- Directness: Does the evidence address the claim directly?
- Reliability: Was it produced using credible methods?
- Independence: Does it provide independent confirmation?
- Consistency: Does it agree with other strong evidence?
- Reproducibility: Can the finding be reproduced?
- Scope: Does the evidence justify the generalization?
- Recency: Is it current enough for the subject?
- Limitations: What does the evidence fail to establish?

A large number of weak sources does not necessarily outweigh one strong primary source.

However, even strong sources must be interpreted within their limitations.

---

17. Research Coverage and Limitations

Be transparent about what the investigation did not establish.

Identify:

- Topics that could not be adequately researched.
- Important sources that were inaccessible.
- Unresolved disagreements.
- Missing historical records.
- Unverified claims.
- Areas where research is still developing.
- Differences between theoretical understanding and practical evidence.

Never describe a research investigation as exhaustive unless its scope and coverage genuinely justify that description.

Use phrases such as:

- This investigation identified...
- The available evidence supports...
- The research did not establish...
- Further verification is needed...
- This is a developing area...

---

18. Special Rule: Discovering Hidden or Overlooked Knowledge

The goal of this Skill includes finding unusual, surprising, and less-discussed knowledge.

However, do not label information as a hidden gem merely because it sounds interesting.

For every proposed hidden gem:

1. Explain the discovery.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Provide its underlying mechanism or evidence.
4. Identify the context where it applies.
5. Explain why it may be overlooked, if that can be supported.
6. Avoid claiming that nobody knows about it.

Prefer:

- Less commonly discussed.
- Frequently misunderstood.
- Often overlooked in beginner explanations.
- Documented but not widely emphasized.

These descriptions are more defensible than claiming that something is secret or unknown.

---

19. Research Quality Self-Audit

Before presenting the final research, evaluate it using this checklist.

Accuracy

- [ ] Important claims have supporting evidence.
- [ ] No facts, dates, quotations, or references were invented.
- [ ] Technical explanations distinguish specifications from implementations.
- [ ] Version-sensitive claims identify their relevant versions.

Sources

- [ ] Important claims use credible sources.
- [ ] Primary sources were prioritized when appropriate.
- [ ] Independent sources were used for cross-verification.
- [ ] Circular citations were avoided.
- [ ] Contradictory evidence was considered.

Reasoning

- [ ] Facts are separated from interpretations and hypotheses.
- [ ] Confidence matches evidence strength.
- [ ] Correlation is not presented as causation.
- [ ] Important limitations are acknowledged.
- [ ] Confirmation bias and selection bias were actively challenged.

Research Depth

- [ ] Important questions were investigated beyond surface-level explanations.
- [ ] Historical claims were checked.
- [ ] Unusual discoveries have supporting evidence.
- [ ] Unsolved problems are distinguished from known limitations.
- [ ] Secondary consequences are not exaggerated.

Transparency

- [ ] Sources are traceable.
- [ ] Uncertainty is clearly communicated.
- [ ] Research limitations are disclosed.
- [ ] No claim of exhaustive coverage is made without justification.

If an important requirement fails, revise the research before presenting it.

---

20. Final Principle

The objective is not to produce the most confident answer.

The objective is to produce the most defensible understanding possible from the available evidence.

A high-quality investigation must make clear:

- What is known.
- Why it is believed.
- How strong the evidence is.
- What remains disputed.
- What is still unknown.
- What further investigation could reveal.

Never sacrifice truth for completeness, confidence, or an impressive explanation.
